# Core Worlds, Gate Hubs, Frontier Corridors, And Dead Zones

Civilization in the Space Adventures universe is not spread evenly. It grows in shapes created by transport.

Where gate density is high, power centralizes. Where wrap traffic is possible but costly, frontier societies remain viable but looser. Where both systems fail or become too dangerous, entire regions fall into practical isolation.

## The Four Major Travel Regions

### Core worlds

Core worlds are the most stable, wealthy, and densely connected systems.

Typical features:

- multiple gate connections or direct access to major gate corridors
- constant high-volume wrap traffic
- deep industrial base for ship service and field maintenance
- political institutions capable of enforcing travel law at scale
- dense data, customs, and security infrastructure

Core worlds are not defined only by population. They are defined by dependable arrival and departure.

## Gate hubs

Gate hubs are the hard junctions of civilization. Some are core worlds, but not all. A gate hub may be a fortress station, an industrial relay system, or a neutral transit city whose importance comes almost entirely from transport geometry.

Typical features:

- one or more strategic hole-teleportation gates
- intense customs and traffic-control regimes
- large escort, salvage, and logistics presence
- political leverage far beyond local population size
- vulnerability to sabotage, blockade, or seizure

If a gate hub fails, regions far away may feel the shock immediately.

## Frontier corridors

Frontier corridors are routes where wrap travel is possible often enough to sustain trade, settlement, and military patrols, but not so cheaply or safely that the region becomes a stable core.

Typical features:

- long chains of repair depots, fuel exchanges, and rescue beacons
- uneven traffic volumes
- stronger role for captains, navigators, and convoy practice
- mixed legal authority across systems
- room for smugglers, dissidents, prospectors, and independent colonies

Frontier corridors are where expansion happens and where order thins out.

## Dead zones

Dead zones are regions that are technically part of space, but not practically part of civilization.

Reasons a region may become a dead zone:

- unstable gravity topology
- dust or debris densities unsafe for reliable wrap navigation
- missing support infrastructure across too many consecutive systems
- ancient conflict damage or abandoned military interdiction fields
- political quarantine
- catastrophic gate loss without replacement corridors

Dead zones are ideal for mysteries, hidden settlements, lost fleets, covert labs, and forgotten wars.

## Why This Geography Exists

The transport systems do not erase distance equally.

- Gates collapse distance at specific engineered nodes.
- Wrap engines reduce distance flexibly but at significant cost and risk.
- System drives dominate local in-system logistics at velocities far below the range where time effects become meaningful.

As a result, civilization spreads like a network, not like a smooth blanket.

## Political Map Logic

This geography produces four recurring political forms:

- central powers anchored in gate-rich cores
- transit authorities that control hub chokepoints
- corridor leagues or frontier protectorates built on wrap commerce
- isolated enclaves surviving beyond dependable state reach

This lets the setting support both imperial-scale coordination and local autonomy.

## Story Guidance

Use each region type differently.

### Core worlds are best for

- diplomacy
- finance and elite politics
- high-density intrigue
- rapid reinforcement and surveillance

### Gate hubs are best for

- chokepoint tension
- customs and espionage
- strategic sabotage
- migration bottlenecks

### Frontier corridors are best for

- merchant adventures
- patrol stories
- colonial friction
- salvage and rescue
- grey-market economies

### Dead zones are best for

- horror
- discovery
- lost-civilization arcs
- black projects
- long-buried military secrets

## Practical Canon Default

Unless a story needs an exception:

- important capitals should sit on or near gate corridors
- prosperous independent systems usually sit on strong wrap routes
- unstable peripheries should be corridor-dependent, not gate-rich
- truly hidden things should live in dead zones or behind artificial denial measures
